Fruits & Vegetables - Shelf Monitoring Solution

10th October, 2026

The Challenge
Challenge name/tagline: Fresh eyes on fresh produce!
Challenge one-liner: use the ceiling cameras of an autonomous store to detect, count and
check the fruits and vegetables on each gondola against the planogram.

Introduction
Fruits and vegetables are the most demanding section of a store for computer vision.
Products are sold loose, with no barcode or packaging. They are piled rather than aligned,
they change shape, size and color as they ripen, and they are constantly hidden by the
hands of shoppers and staff.
At the same time, it is the section where knowing the state of the shelf matters most,
since produce is perishable:
Empty gondola » lost sales » unhappy customers
Overstocked gondola » products left too long on the shelf » waste
Wrong product in the wrong place » confused customers » wrong prices
Today, these checks depend on staff walking the store. Labelling this kind of data is itself
hard and subjective: counting bananas in a pile is a challenge even for a person. Across
hundreds of stores and thousands of cameras, nobody can label every image, every day.
So, the solution must learn to see the shelf without being told what is right. Only a small,
hidden validation set has been annotated, so that all solutions can be compared fairly.

The core of the challenge
One of the first steps towards a store that knows its own shelves is to recognise, count
and check the products on each gondola, continuously and without human help.
Our company operates autonomous stores fitted with ceiling cameras and has created a
dataset specifically for this challenge. It contains images from 14 cameras installed over
the Fruits & Vegetables section of Continente Bom Dia S. Romão in Leiria — the largest
autonomous store in the world, powered by Sensei — extracted during one full day of
operation. Each camera sees one or multiple gondola modules.

Gondolas and products were chosen so that products can be recognised generically (e.g.
banana, orange), with no need to know brands or packaging. The file planogram.json holds
the planogram active at the start of the day for each module (for each product, its id, its
name and the image coordinates of its planogram area).

The planogram file
planogram.json tells, for each of the 14 cameras, which product should occupy each area
of the shelf at the start of the day. It describes 152 planogram areas in total, with this
structure:
{"Frutas e Legumes": {"cameras": {"6215": {"products": [
{"area_id": "6215_2",
"product_id": "11908",
"product_name": "BANANA CAT I CNT KG",
"image_coordinates": {"top_left": [299, 350], "top_right": [464, 347],
"bottom_right": [479, 438], "bottom_left": [331,
438]}}
]}}}}

Frutas e Legumes: the store section.
cameras: one entry per camera, keyed by the camera id (e.g. 6215).
products: the list of planogram areas seen by that camera, each with its area_id (a
unique id made of the camera id and an area number, e.g. 6215_2), product_id,
product_name and image_coordinates.
image_coordinates: the four corners of the area in the camera image, as [x, y] pixel
positions measured from the top-left corner of the image. Areas are four-sided shapes
rather than rectangles, because of the camera perspective.
A few details are worth keeping in mind:
The same product can occupy several areas. For example, BANANA CAT I CNT KG has
six areas on camera 6215, so stock should be counted per area and then added up per
product.
Some coordinates are negative, meaning the area extends beyond the edge of the
image and is only partly visible (e.g. the top shelf of camera 6383).
Product names are in Portuguese, as in the store's product catalogue. Names ending in
KG are sold loose, by weight, so a unit is a single fruit or vegetable. Names with a
weight (e.g. 500 G, EMB. 3KG) are packaged, so a unit is one package.

The goal is to produce an application that, for each image, solves as many of the following
tasks as feasible:
1. Detection and classification: find the products in the image and name them.
2. Stock counting: estimate how much of each product is in each planogram area, as an
item count or a fill level.
3. Planogram comparison: say, for each area, whether it is compliant, low, empty or
holding the wrong product.
The images are split into three sets. The train set and the test set are shared with
candidates and have no annotations. The validation set is kept hidden from candidates
during the training and programming stage, and is the only set with ground-truth
annotations. When requested, candidates run their own code on the validation images
and submit the outputs, which are then compared automatically with the annotations.

The annotation format
The ground truth of the validation set is stored in annotations.json. It covers 26 images,
one or two per camera, and every planogram area in each image (289 annotated areas in
total), with this structure:
{"6215": {
"1751052241.jpg": {
"6215_2": {"planogram_compliant": true,
"stock_level": "half",
"stock_value": 0.5}
}}}

Camera id (e.g. 6215): the same ids as in planogram.json.
Image file name (e.g. 1751052241.jpg): the capture time of the image, as a Unix
timestamp.
area_id (e.g. 6215_2): the planogram area, exactly as in planogram.json, where its
product and image coordinates can be found.
planogram_compliant: true if the product actually shown in the area is the one the
planogram expects, false otherwise.
stock_level: how much product is in the area, in words: empty, half or full. The same
level is given as a number in stock_value: 0 for empty, 0.5 for half and 1 for full.

Candidates submit their output in exactly the same format: one entry per image and per
area_id, with planogram_compliant, stock_level and stock_value filled in by their
application. To compare an output with the ground truth, each area is matched by camera
id, image file name and area_id, and then planogram_compliant and stock_value are
compared as described below.

How the solution will be appraised
The outputs on the validation set are compared automatically with the annotations, area
by area. The scoring follows the way a member of the store staff would use the
application.
Picture an employee starting a shift. They open the app, see the stock level of every
gondola and every planogram mismatch, and plan their work from it: what to restock first,
what to move back to its place, what to leave alone. Every wrong piece of information has
a cost:
False alarm » staff walk to a shelf that needed nothing » time lost
Missed problem » empty or wrong shelf left in front of customers » lost sales
Repeated errors » frustration » staff stop trusting the app
The scoring therefore rewards information staff can act on and penalises each error by
the trouble it would cause them.
The level of achievement will be measured area by area, adding and subtracting points
according to the consequences for the staff¹:
1. Product identification of each planogram area, compared with planogram_compliant:
the answer is correct when it states rightly whether the product actually shown in the
image is the one the planogram expects:
a. Correct identification; +1 point.
b. Wrong identification; −2 points, since staff go there to arrange products and there
is nothing to arrange.
2. Stock level of each planogram area, compared with stock_value (0 for empty, 0.5 for
half, 1 for full): a lower value than the annotation is below the real shelf stock, a higher
value is above it:
a. Correct stock level; +1 point.
b. Stock level below the real shelf stock; −2 points, since staff go there to replenish
and the shelf is already full.

In addition, the user interface and user experience (UI/UX) of the application will be

in points between candidates: among them, the one with the best UI/UX ranks first.

Future potential and benefits
Sensei operates a fleet of autonomous stores, each with cameras over every gondola. A
solution that needs no labels can be switched on in a new store with no extra hardware
and no labelling effort.
Creating a tool that could "watch" every gondola, all day long, could provide an insight
into:
when each product needs restocking, before the gondola runs empty
which products stay too long on the shelf and end up as waste
how well each store follows its planogram, without manual audits
